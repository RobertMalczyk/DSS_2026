================================================================
HOW WE TAUGHT A GENRE CLASSIFIER TO SURVIVE A NOISY WORLD
A development story, told in chronological order
================================================================

WHERE WE STARTED
--------------------------------------------------------------

The Training_model project already had a frozen baseline. A music
genre classifier (RBF-SVM on a 48-dim feature vector: 20 MFCC means,
20 MFCC stds, plus mean/std of spectral centroid, rolloff, bandwidth
and zero-crossing rate) was being run on a 250-track dataset of
jazz / metal / pop, evaluated under StratifiedGroupKFold so no track
ever appeared in both train and test of the same fold.

The story this baseline told was sharp:

  A  clean -> clean              0.988  (the upper bound)
  B  noisy -> noisy              0.912  (noise-aware training)
  C  clean -> noisy              0.344  (the cliff)

Training on clean and asking the model to predict on contaminated
audio dropped accuracy from 99 % to 34 %. The noise model itself was
nasty -- mains hum, switching tones, broadband EMI, impulsive bursts,
1/f drift, 8-bit quantisation, plus clipping -- at SNR -10 dB.

A series of "test-time denoiser" experiments (D / E / F / G) tried
to close the gap by filtering the noisy audio before showing it to
the clean-trained classifier:

  D  clean -> filtered_AI            0.404   (AI denoiser)
  E  clean -> filtered_1st           0.340   (classical 1st-stage)
  F  clean -> filtered_2nd           0.436   (classical + AI 2nd-stage)
  G  clean -> adaptivems_6th         0.492   (adaptive mean-subtraction)

Two patterns showed up in those numbers that mattered for everything
that came after.

First, no test-time filter recovered more than ~23 % of the A-C gap.
Training on noisy (B at 0.912) closed ~88 % of it. Filtering at test
time was a much weaker lever than letting the model see the noise
during training.

Second -- and more interesting -- four of the five clean-trained
cross-conditions (C / D / E / G) didn't degrade gracefully. They
collapsed into a single dump-target genre. The dump shifted with
the filter (pop for C, jazz for D, metal for E, pop for G) but in
each case one genre took nearly everything and the other two went
to roughly zero. Only F was the exception: its per-genre numbers
sat in 0.33-0.57 range, with no genre disappearing. F was the only
filter that produced a real three-way prediction, even though its
mean accuracy (0.436) was lower than G's. That distinction --
"highest accuracy" vs "no collapse" -- is two different problems.

A separate sidequest had also tried bandlimiting the input to 3 kHz
before extracting features (variants E' and F'). E' lifted the
weakest filter off the chance floor (E 0.340 -> E' 0.404), F' barely
moved in mean (F 0.436 -> F' 0.456) but its per-fold std halved.
The lesson: deliberately throwing away high-frequency content can
help, but where you place the cutoff matters more than whether you
have one. We will use that lesson again in Chapter 3.

A second sidequest had compared classifiers on the same 48-dim
features. SVM's clean / noisy numbers were 0.988 / 0.876; XGBoost
managed 0.980 / 0.852 with the same folds. SVM also produced a
smaller noise-induced gap. So on this hand-crafted feature
representation, SVM was the classifier to beat -- the lever with
real upside was *richer features*, not a fancier model. We will
return to that lesson too.


CHAPTER 1: A NEW FILTER ARRIVES
--------------------------------------------------------------

A new filter, the "ComponentMatched 7th stage", appeared in the
sibling project. It was a four-stage chain: a 25 Hz high-pass to
kill 1/f drift, narrow notches at the mains harmonics 50/100/150/200
Hz and the switching tone near 4410 Hz, then an adaptive Wiener
stage to attack the broadband residual.

We added it to the experiment matrix in the same shape as G:

  H  clean -> componentmatched_7th   0.560

This was the new best test-time filter (33.5 % gap recovery, vs G's
23 %). But the per-genre breakdown was familiar: jazz 0.90, metal
0.00, pop 0.77 -- the classifier had collapsed into a two-genre
prediction, treating metal as if it didn't exist. Higher accuracy,
same structural failure.


CHAPTER 2: MATCH FEATURES TO THE FILTER'S GEOMETRY
--------------------------------------------------------------

The clean-trained model was failing on filtered audio because the
training and test feature distributions were misaligned. The clean
audio it learned from had peaks at the mains harmonics; the filtered
audio it was tested on had those peaks carved out by the notches.
MFCC bands sitting on top of those frequencies behaved differently
in train and test.

The fix we tried: replicate the filter's *linear* stages on the
clean audio before extracting features. Same high-pass at 25 Hz,
same notches at 50/100/150/200 and 4410 Hz. Train and test would
then both sit on a spectrum with the same five carve-outs.

  H_pf  clean_pf -> 7th_pf          0.572   (+0.012 vs H)

The win was real but tiny. The notches are roughly 1.4 Hz wide --
extremely narrow compared to MFCC mel bands that span tens to
hundreds of Hz. Removing five thin lines barely shifted any band's
energy. The dominant clean-to-filtered distribution shift wasn't
coming from the notches at all. It was coming from the adaptive
Wiener stage, which applies a data-dependent gain that suppresses
low-SNR bins and reshapes the whole spectral envelope of the music.


CHAPTER 3: FOLLOW THE FILTER'S PASSBAND
--------------------------------------------------------------

We could not replay Wiener on clean audio without measuring the
noise (which the rules of the project forbid). But the filter's
design told us something useful: below the switching tone at
4410 Hz, music dominates the noise, so Wiener stays near unity --
the filtered audio in 25-4000 Hz is essentially the music with a
few thin lines missing. Above 4 kHz, broadband EMI tends to
dominate Wiener's behaviour, and the filtered output diverges
sharply from clean.

So we extended the matched prefilter into a true band-pass: the
same HP and notches, plus a Butterworth low-pass at 4000 Hz, applied
identically to clean (training) and filtered (test) audio.

  H_bf4000  clean_bf4000 -> 7th_bf4000     0.660  (49.1 % recovery)

Per-genre: jazz 0.92, metal 0.53, pop 0.53. Only the second
cross-condition in the project's whole history (after F) to produce
a real three-way prediction on filtered audio with no genre near
zero -- and at much higher overall accuracy than F's 0.436. We
confirmed the band edge mattered by also running a 5500 Hz cutoff
*above* the switching tone:

  H_bf4000  LP at 4000 Hz  (below switching)    0.660 +/- 0.078
  H_bf5500  LP at 5500 Hz  (above switching)    0.632 +/- 0.118

H_bf5500 readmitted the heavily Wiener-attenuated 4-5.5 kHz band
into the feature support; per-fold std almost doubled, mean dropped
0.028, and pop slid back toward a dump (0.37). The cutoff has to
sit just below the switching tone, not just above it. Same lesson
as the earlier 3 kHz E'/F' sidequest, this time applied to the
right filter and with the cutoff at the right place.

Cost: the clean-on-clean anchor dropped from 0.988 to ~0.95 -- the
LP throws away high-frequency timbre that helps with metal on
truly-clean audio. Net: +10 pts on the cross-condition, -4 pts on
the upper bound. Clear win.


CHAPTER 4: USE WHAT WE KNOW ABOUT THE *KINDS* OF NOISE
--------------------------------------------------------------

We had a list of seven noise sources from the project's analysis
documents: mains, switching, broadband EMI, impulsive bursts, 1/f
drift, 8-bit quantisation, clipping. The prefilter handled mains,
switching and drift. What remained?

  - Impulsive bursts: short transients, ~3 per second, ~20 ms each.
    Mean-and-std aggregation over a window's frames is sensitive
    to these spikes -- one burst frame inflates the std and biases
    the mean. So we replaced the aggregator: median and MAD
    (median absolute deviation, scaled by 1.4826) instead of mean
    and std. Same dimensionality, but burst-resistant.

  - In-band broadband EMI: stationary background noise. Stationary
    contributions average to roughly zero in the time-derivative
    of MFCCs. So we added Delta-MFCC and DeltaDelta-MFCC, classical
    robust-ASR features, computed per frame and aggregated with
    the same median + MAD.

  - Clipping: gives a similar signature to bursts (impulsive amplitude
    spikes), so the median-MAD aggregator addresses it for free.

None of these required peeking at the noisy files. They were a
direct response to the *types* of noise the dataset contained.


CHAPTER 5: WHAT MAKES JAZZ, METAL AND POP DIFFERENT (BEYOND TIMBRE)?
--------------------------------------------------------------

The 48-dim baseline was almost entirely a timbre descriptor: MFCCs
and brightness statistics. But genres are also rhythmic, harmonic
and dynamic creatures.

We added four small blocks of musically-informed features:

  - chroma_stft (12 pitch classes): metal's power chords concentrate
    on root + 5th; jazz's extended chords spread across the chroma;
    pop has clear major/minor tonics. Below 4 kHz is where
    fundamentals and first overtones live, so the bf4000 prefilter
    actually *helps* chroma, not hurts it.

  - spectral_contrast: peak-vs-valley energy across sub-bands. Metal
    distortion fills the spectral valleys (low contrast). Jazz lines
    are clean (high contrast). Direct genre discriminator.

  - spectral_flatness: tonal-vs-noisy ratio. Distorted guitar is
    near-noisy; saxophone is near-tonal.

  - RMS, crest factor, onset rate: dynamic and rhythmic descriptors.
    Pop is heavily compressed (low RMS variance). Metal has dense,
    fast onsets. Jazz has wide dynamic range and looser pulse.

Each frame-level feature was aggregated with median + MAD; onset
rate is one scalar per window. The vector grew from 48 dims to 173:
60 MFCC blocks, 8 scalar spectral, 24 chroma, 14 contrast, 6
flatness/RMS/crest, plus onset rate.


CHAPTER 6: ALL TOGETHER -- FOUR EXPERIMENTS, ONE FEATURE STACK
--------------------------------------------------------------

We ran all four pairings under the same v2 feature stack and the
same fold assignments:

  A_v2  clean -> clean               0.964 +/- 0.015     (sanity)
  B_v2  noisy -> noisy               0.916 +/- 0.015     (sanity)
  H_v2  clean -> 7th                 0.796 +/- 0.054     <-- new best
  M_v2  noisy -> 7th (the new ask)   0.332 +/- 0.010     (collapse)

Two surprises in one table.

The first was the headline: H_v2 = 0.796 was a huge jump on the
cross-condition. It recovers 70 % of the original A-C cliff (versus
the previous best of 49 %). All three genres land above 0.75 -- the
single-genre collapse that had haunted every test-time filter from
the start, including H_bf4000, was finally gone. The combination of
matched prefilter + robust aggregation + temporal derivatives +
musical features gave the classifier enough redundancy that no
single noise pattern could knock it off any one genre.

The second was the lesson: M_v2 = 0.332 is exactly chance. The
noisy-trained model picked jazz for ~80 % of the test tracks and
got only the actual-jazz tracks right. Why?

A model trained on noisy audio doesn't just learn to *ignore* the
noise -- it learns to use the noise as part of its feature vocabulary.
Pop has the heaviest spectral content above 1 kHz, so it absorbs the
most broadband EMI energy; metal has the loudest peaks, so it clips
most often; jazz has softer transients, so it shows the smallest
burst rate. Those are real, exploitable correlations *within the
noisy distribution*. When the test audio comes through Stage 7,
those noise-correlated patterns are gone -- exactly the features
the noisy-trained classifier had come to rely on -- and it collapses.

Three patterns aligned to make this clear:

  - B works (noisy -> noisy):  noise present in both train and test.
  - H works (clean -> 7th):    noise absent in train, mostly removed
                               in test. The filter aims to recover the
                               clean distribution, and it succeeds well
                               enough that a clean-trained model
                               generalises.
  - M fails (noisy -> 7th):    train and test live on opposite sides
                               of the noise -- the worst pairing.


THE FINAL SCOREBOARD
--------------------------------------------------------------

Anchors (sanity, not "recovery"):

  A    clean -> clean       baseline 48-dim                    0.988
  B    noisy -> noisy       baseline 48-dim                    0.912
  A_v2 clean -> clean       v2 173-dim                         0.964
  B_v2 noisy -> noisy       v2 173-dim                         0.916

Test-time filters, baseline 48-dim features:

  C    clean -> noisy            (no filter)              0.344       -
  E    clean -> filtered_1st     (classical 1st)          0.340   -0.6 %
  D    clean -> filtered_AI      (AI denoiser)            0.404    9.3 %
  F    clean -> filtered_2nd     (classical+AI 2nd)       0.436   14.3 %
  G    clean -> adaptivems_6th   (adaptive-MS 6th)        0.492   23.0 %
  H    clean -> componentmatched_7th  (component-matched) 0.560   33.5 %

  E'   filtered_1st under 3 kHz LPF                       0.404    9.3 %
  F'   filtered_2nd under 3 kHz LPF                       0.456   17.4 %

Stage-7-aware feature variants on the same 7th-stage test audio:

  H_pf      48-dim, matched HP+notches                    0.572   35.4 %
  H_bf5500  48-dim, HP+notches+LP at 5500 Hz              0.632   44.7 %
  H_bf4000  48-dim, HP+notches+LP at 4000 Hz              0.660   49.1 %
  H_v2      173-dim full v2 stack                         0.796   70.2 %

The user-proposed alternative training pairing (this session):

  M_v2  noisy -> 7th, v2 features                         0.332   chance

Classifier sidequest on the original 48-dim features (harsher-noise era):

  SVM(rbf, C=10)         clean / noisy        0.988 / 0.876
  XGBoost(n=400, d=5)    clean / noisy        0.980 / 0.852

Recovery is `(acc - C) / (A - C)` on the frozen baseline anchors.


WHAT WE LEARNED
--------------------------------------------------------------

1. When the test pipeline includes a denoiser, train and test feature
   distributions must end up on the same side of the noise. Stage 7
   recovers (an approximation of) the clean distribution, so train
   on clean. Train on noisy only when test audio is also noisy.

2. Matching the linear part of a filter (notches, HP) on the clean
   training audio is essentially free, but the gain is limited
   because narrow notches barely move any MFCC band. The bigger
   structural mismatch is the data-dependent part of the filter
   (here, Wiener), which we worked around by restricting the feature
   support to the filter's high-confidence passband (LP at 4 kHz,
   just below the switching tone).

3. Robustness comes from feature design, not just hyperparameter
   tuning. Replacing mean+std with median+MAD costs nothing and
   handles bursts and clipping. Adding Delta and DeltaDelta MFCCs
   neutralises stationary in-band noise. Adding chroma and spectral
   contrast adds a second axis of class discrimination that timbre
   alone misses.

4. Domain knowledge (where the noise comes from, what makes genres
   musically distinct) outperforms brute hyperparameter search on a
   small dataset. Each feature-design step here can be traced back
   to a single sentence in the noise spec or a single musical fact
   about the genres.

5. The single-genre collapse pattern is a signal about the model,
   not the genres. Every collapse we saw -- pop dump, jazz dump,
   metal disappearance -- meant the classifier had latched on to a
   feature that broke under the test-time perturbation. Each fix
   that changed the dump target without breaking it was a partial
   solution; the full v2 stack was the first thing that broke the
   collapse outright.
